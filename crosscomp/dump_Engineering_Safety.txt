[Who is centered]: Researchers refining acceptance-measurement tools; riders' open-ended comments about the vehicle's interior temperature, a human safety operator's talkativeness, and jerky braking are treated as secondary, supplementary evidence rather than the paper's main contribution.
[Relevance to argument]: This paper is a strong methodological complement to Palatinus et al., offering real riders in realistic though low-speed conditions rather than a simulator, and its qualitative finding that a human safety operator meaningfully shaped riders' perceptions is a rare moment in this literature where a rider's relational experience of the ride surfaces at all, even though the researchers treat it as a confounding variable to control for rather than something to design around.
[Gap or limitation]: The sample was self-selected, young, and drawn from visitors to a public science event riding a slow, twelve kilometer-per-hour shuttle on private land with a safety operator aboard, and the authors themselves caution against generalizing beyond this specific group or to sustained, repeated use over time.
[Methodology/study type]: Real-world field pilot study surveying 125 self-selected riders immediately after an actual ride in a shared electric autonomous shuttle at a public science event in Berlin, combining exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis with qualitative coding of open-ended comments.

### [Engineering/Safety] Nordhoff, S., Stapel, J., van Arem, B., & Happee, R. (2020) - Passenger opinions of the perceived safety and interaction with automated shuttles: A test ride study with 'hidden' safety steward
[Sub-theme]: Acceptance & passenger experience
[Central claim]: The paper argues that passengers' perceived safety in a driverless shuttle is assembled from concrete vehicle- and environment-level cues, low speed, smooth braking, the emergency button, operation on a controlled route, and that the successful integration of automated shuttles therefore depends on engineering these perceptions and on managing how outside pedestrians and cyclists interact with the vehicle.
[Key concepts or framework]: Perceived safety: a passenger's subjective feeling of safety, distinct from actual safety, decomposed here into vehicle-, individual-, and environment-related factors and treated as the central precondition for acceptance. 
Hidden safety steward: a human operator disguised as a 'minute writer' so riders experience the shuttle as genuinely driverless, keeping the study's safety net from inflating perceived safety. 
Incidental or vulnerable road users: pedestrians and cyclists who never chose to interact with the shuttle but must negotiate it anyway, whose acceptance the paper treats as a second necessary condition for deployment. 
Testing behavior: the tendency of passengers to press the emergency button and of outside road users to step in front of the shuttle to probe its limits, which the paper reads as distrust, curiosity, or a problem to be managed.
[Who is centered]: Passengers and, secondarily, outside pedestrians and cyclists, but centered as bearers of a perceived safety to be measured and managed, alongside the manufacturers, operators, and researchers who decide how to respond; when road users test the shuttle, the paper's proposed fixes run toward protecting the technology's safety and efficiency benefits, up to having the vehicle's sensors flag non-compliant road users to the police.
[Relevance to argument]: This is the acceptance sub-theme's most community-adjacent anchor, real riders in a real moving vehicle, whose relational cues such as sharing the ride and trusting a responsible transport operator briefly surface, yet it still frames the whole encounter as perceived safety to be optimized for successful deployment. Its remedies for road users who negotiate with the shuttle run toward educating them, redesigning the vehicle for compliance, or sanctioning them, rather than accommodating their needs, showing exactly where the mainstream approaches a service or relational framing and then turns back.
[Gap or limitation]: Passengers only imagined interacting with the shuttle as pedestrians or cyclists rather than actually doing so, the vehicle ran slowly on a controlled private campus with a hidden steward aboard, and the sample was self-selected from mobility-project and meetup participants, so the authors caution that perceived safety in faster, genuinely driverless, mixed-traffic conditions remains untested.
[Methodology/study type]: Mixed-method accompanied test-ride study triangulating qualitative interviews conducted during the ride with a post-ride questionnaire, across 119 respondents on 62 rides averaging about 37 minutes in a low-speed EasyMile shuttle sharing mixed traffic on the EUREF campus in Berlin, with a hidden steward on board.
