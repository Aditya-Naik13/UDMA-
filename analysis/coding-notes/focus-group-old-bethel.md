# Coding notes: Old Bethel community weaver interview (Introductions and Persona)

- Source file: transcripts/Focus Groups/Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx
- Site: Old Bethel food pantry (church pantry, Warren Township area, Indianapolis; informant also works St. Vincent de Paul on 30th and Windsor Village pantries)
- Type: single-informant interview plus persona-building and stakeholder-mapping exercise, dated 4 November 2024. Informant is a "community weaver" / community advocate, not a pantry client. This is one of the older community-weaver interviews.
- Language: English
- Format / locator scheme: docx, `MM:SS` timestamp lines, no speaker labels. One interviewee (the community weaver) and two student interviewers, plus references to a faculty lead. Locator = `file, MM:SS`. Two passages are marked "Deleted" in the source (redacted client criminal-history detail at 56:37 and 57:06); not quotable.
- Participants present: 1 community weaver, 2 student researchers
- Read in full: yes

Note on use: rich for anchors 1 to 4 and for the "without community participation"
strand. All of it is one advocate's account, much of it his characterization
of clients' feelings and a composite persona ("Janet"), not clients speaking
directly. Treat persona statements as the weaver's informed inference, not
client testimony.

## 1. Values

- Help as a universal obligation and a self-perpetuating cycle.
  - "this is universal, help is universal. To extend your hand, I think that's going to be your biggest test and your biggest asset." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 13:28)
  - "You give back, they give back. So that's why I say that synchronicity, it always comes back." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 10:47)
- The role names itself relational: "community weaver" is the current term, but
  the informant reads it as "community advocate" and does political and social
  connective work far beyond food.
  - "I'll be your community weaver, as they call it now. They have two or three terms now... it's really just community advocate." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 08:25)
  - "I would kind of be that conduit to whatever y'all would do." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 04:58)
- A church pantry is trusted because it is read as compassionate and
  non-judgmental, and open across denominations.
  - "they feel comfortable with Old Bethel because they know it's a church pantry. So they know more, they're more compassionate, more understanding, more empathetic." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:20:40)
  - "old Beth was super cool. Could be Baptist, Catholic, whatever, and still come." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 39:57)
- Shame and pride are the defining emotional texture of pantry use, across
  cultures, and they suppress people from asking for what they are entitled to.
  - "Want to help, need to help, but actually ashamed to ask for help. And actually don't know where to go." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 41:08)
  - "one of my Burmese family said. My family would be ashamed. I would be ashamed if they knew I was coming here." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 55:05)
  - "welfare, food, you're in the pantry, you're dirty. It's almost like the mental ill still, stigma." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 55:51)
- The pantry as a social and dignity space, not only a food source. Clients
  come partly to see each other.
  - "that's almost a socialization thing, because they see the other people... Then they go to the other pantry together, just like they go to Goodwill's and the little save-a-lives together." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 30:25)
- Independence as the stated client goal, sometimes voiced as never wanting to
  come back.
  - "long as I can get on my feet, I don't have to see y'all again." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:12:22)
  - "that's the biggest goal I think they have is independence. To not to be able to rely on a pantry or snap or welfare or anything." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:16:18)

## 2. Capabilities and Constraints

- Capability: the community weaver is himself dense infrastructure. Twenty
  years across three pantries, drives donation routes, recruits interpreters
  from among clients, refers people to township trustees and health programs.
  - "really I'm involved with three different pantries. Because of the hours and the flexibility I have now since I retired, so I was able to kind of do all three." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 33:09)
  - "one of those is actually one of our interpreters for the Burmese. She's gonna be an interpreter now... eventually we get somebody that can kind of speak English and wants to help out and they'll stay." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 20:45)
- Capability: multilingual refugee clients. The informant frames their
  language range as an asset against local monolingualism.
  - "almost every one of them that's from Guadalajara, they speak six, seven, five, six languages. We speak one. So we gotta give it to them... cause they feel useless over here, or hopeless." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 11:50)
- Capability: word-of-mouth networks that grew the pantry and run informal
  exchange.
  - "They quick with the word of mouth. And that's the original internet right there." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:13:45)
  - clients set up a surplus-swap in the back: "in the back, around the corner, we can exchange some stuff... you just put stuff that you don't want on it" (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:13:52)
- Capability: resourcefulness in use. Clients route surplus vegetables to
  livestock, cook cabbage into multi-day meals, share big squashes by
  language group.
  - "she has horses. So she took all these cabbages... That was a lot." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 48:36)
- Capability/constraint pair: clients want work but childcare cost exceeds the
  wage, so they cannot take the job.
  - "she's looking for a job and hasn't found one because the babysitter or the daycare would overspend her. So she can't afford a babysitter, got to stay home." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 37:51)
- Constraint: transportation is named as the single biggest obstacle,
  repeatedly. No bus stop near the pantry, no sidewalks, walking with a box
  and children.
  - "that's one of the biggest obstacles... is the transportation to the pantry." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 42:34)
  - "she's got to carry that box with her two kids, usually... crossing that street, ain't no sidewalks around here." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 39:57 and 01:09:28)
  - "They ain't getting no Uber, no Lyft. Sometimes when they catch that bus, that's $2. But that Uber and Lyft, that's $25, $30." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:06:45)
- Constraint: language barrier at the computerized intake.
  - "that's the big thing in the computer, the intake is the language barrier." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 19:22)
- Constraint: everything has moved online, and many clients have no phone,
  wifi, or the literacy for a digital job application.
  - "You'd be surprised how many people don't have a bank account. phone or internet or Wi-Fi." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 33:36)
  - "He said, there's no paper application? I said, no, I haven't seen one in years." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:27:21)
- Constraint: demand tripled, straining the model.
  - "125, that would be a good Saturday. Now it's 350, 400 on a Saturday." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 06:05)

## 3. Meanings and Practices

- Meaning: the pantry trip is routine and a source of stability. People come
  the same day, the same hour, "like coming to a job."
  - "It's almost like, yeah, coming to a job almost." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:00:58)
  - "It might be something too for people that don't necessarily have that stability in their life... That's what I said. Since I got something to go to." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:02:11)
- Practice: multi-pantry circuits. Regular clients visit two or three pantries
  a week to assemble enough food.
  - "she's coming to at least three pantries, two or three times a week to supplement her food... she's been doing it for like five years." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 27:26)
- Practice: walking with a stroller as a cart. The persona wheels a box and a
  toddler home past the Dollar General, cutting through the parking lot
  because there is no safe route.
  - "she's rolling that stroller down here with that little kid, put her little box on there, balance it." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:08:57)
- Practice: the weaver runs a personal delivery route for homebound neighbors.
  - "I have a neighbor that I usually take a box to she's 85 and a cancer survivor. They can't really drive no more... so I get her a nice little box." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:10:04)
- Practice: the Christmas box delivery operation scaled from a few drivers to
  about twenty.
  - "it used to be like 50, 60. 500, they done signed up for that Christmas box... now I think it's like 20 of us has gotta go out and deliver." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 49:04)
- Practice: information moves by mouth and by table. Health, SNAP, and Medicare
  sign-up tables now run at the pantry every week.
  - "they're having a table there every week and people signing up for all these, not just Obamacare, but Medicaid." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 51:06)

## 4. Misalignment

- Tension: the shift from a choice or "shopping" model to a pre-packed box,
  driven by volume. Efficient and necessary at 400 families, but it removed
  the browsing, comparison, and selection clients valued.
  - Value at stake: flexibility for variable need, and client agency and
    dignity.
  - "in the old days they would come in and shop, it took much longer. And like 30 points... Now we just put it in the box and move on. Because... the volume got so much from 100 to 350, 400, we couldn't just keep letting them stay in there." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 15:03)
  - "people still think they in Kroger or something. Checking the prices... that tendency to look and see best one" (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 15:33)
- Tension: visit frequency rationed from weekly or twice-weekly down to twice
  a month, against clients whose need is continuous.
  - Value at stake: flexibility for variable need.
  - "Now they've kind of restricted it now. So once every you know, you do two times a month. But when it was like you could do come once a week or twice a week... half the people twice a week. the need was that strong." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 27:00)
- Tension: card, number, and ID intake. Streamlined from the old
  immigration-paper regime, but it still gates entry on producing documents
  and a photo ID at a computer.
  - Value at stake: equitable access, relational trust.
  - "everybody's got a card, their number, show their ID, move on." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 14:46)
  - the earlier regime: "it used to be a whole thing about their paper, nationalization papers, immigration paper... their passport, all that. That was like 10 years ago." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 26:33)
- Tension: adding a bus stop near the pantry requires IndyGo to run a
  feasibility and sustainability study first, so an obvious access fix is
  stalled in process.
  - Value at stake: equitable access, and the pace mismatch between
    institutional procedure and lived urgency.
  - "I've already talked to people in Indigo... they always do a sustainability study... Feasibility, sustainability, gotta do it. It's almost like DEI, they got to do it now. So we're waiting on that." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 42:11)
- Tension: a data-collection rule stopped the pantry from recording client
  nationality, which the weaver had been using to plan interpreter coverage.
  - Value at stake: flexibility for variable need. A privacy or compliance
    rule cut against a practical service adaptation.
  - "we was doing it at first when we started to actually kind of check on the nationality thing, but I think they said we couldn't do that or something. But I was just curious... getting interpreters for each." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 25:51)
- Tension: the new pantry building is being designed as a drive-through for
  flow.
  - Value at stake: this optimizes throughput; whether it also serves the
    carless walker or the socialization function is unresolved in the
    transcript. Coder note: the weaver frames it positively ("more like your
    flow") and says a walk-up lane is also planned.
  - "It's almost gonna be the drive-through like I do at Urban Reef. So it's gonna be string-like, more like your flow." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 31:17)
- Tension: institutions renaming away from "service" and "community". The
  weaver notices FSSA becoming "family and social resources" and reads
  significance into it.
  - Value at stake: community participation, and the social-service framing.
  - "Family, social, resources. I'm like, wow. They've actually went to that now." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 52:17)
- Tension: hiring has moved to online resumes and applications, closing the
  walk-in route older clients knew.
  - Value at stake: equitable access to work.
  - "can't you just walk in and ask for a job?... That ain't how it works no more. But then old days, that's what they did." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:27:50)

## 5. Provocation

- Speculative prompt present: no. No AV, automation, or future-mobility prompt
  was asked in this interview.
- Adjacent, not AV: the transportation-solution conversation is entirely
  low-tech. The weaver is lobbying IndyGo for a bus stop, and names gas cards
  and bus passes as the resources to build out.
  - "Resource for like bus passes and stuff like that. We're gonna start trying to do that too. Gas cards stuff like that like urban league." (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 54:12)
  - "vouchers for food to go to Kroger to get gas cards if you for transportation" (Old Bethel Interview Transcript.docx, 01:24:42)

## Cross-bucket notes

- This transcript is a strong statement of the "arrives without participation"
  theme, but the actor imposing decisions here is the food-bank network and
  the funders, not a tech vendor: box model, twice-a-month cap, ID card,
  no-nationality-data rule, and the bus-stop study requirement are all
  described as coming down onto the pantry and its clients.
- The composite persona "Janet" (30s, three kids, one possibly autistic, walks
  from Braeburn, ashamed to ask, referred to township trustees) is the
  team's own artifact. Use it as the team's synthesized picture of the median
  client, clearly labeled as such, not as a real person.
- "the original internet right there" (word of mouth) is a quotable line for
  the capability argument: the community already runs a distribution and
  information network the formal system does not see.
- The weaver's own biography (retired, veteran with a bus pass for life,
  daughter is a doctor who goes in two days a week) is the counterpoint: he
  has the resources and mobility his clients lack, and that is why he can
  weave.
