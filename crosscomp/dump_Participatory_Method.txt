### [Participatory Method] Baumann, K., Caldwell, B., Bar, F., & Stokes, B. (2018) - Community storytelling for speculative urban technologies.
[Central claim]: This paper argues that speculative urban technologies (including self-driving cars) should be co-designed with residents through long-term community storytelling, because doing so builds a community's lasting capacity to shape its own technological future rather than having that future imposed on it.
[Key concepts or framework]: Participatory design fiction: combining real community participation with fictional/speculative technology concepts, so residents react to and reshape a not-yet-real tech.
Infrastructures of the imagination: the idea that a community's ability to picture and steer its own future is something you can deliberately build up, like infrastructure.
Community storytelling: residents author their own scenarios about future tech, instead of researchers authoring them on their behalf.
[Relevance to argument]: This is one of the closest existing precedents for exactly what we did - residents used workshops and a speculative self-driving car concept to shape technology on their own terms, in a community under outside pressure. It directly supports our thread by showing that a speculative AV provocation run through community workshops is a proven way to surface what a community values, which is the core of our "arrives without their participation" critique.
[Gap or limitation]: As a one-page extended abstract, it gives little detail on the analysis method or on whether the workshops produced any lasting change for the neighborhood, so it can't fully evidence the durability it claims.

### [Participatory Method] Severs, R., Wu, J., Diels, C., Harrow, D., Singleton, J., & Winsor, R. (2022) - Imagining an inclusive future for shared autonomous vehicle interiors: A participatory design workshop study
[Central claim]: This paper argues that transport-excluded groups hold specific, identifiable design needs for shared autonomous vehicles that mainstream AV design misses, and that participatory workshops are the necessary method for surfacing those needs, since these groups have no direct experience of the technology to draw on otherwise.
[Key concepts or framework]: Journey mapping: participants walk through what a typical trip currently looks like for them, exposing where existing mobility breaks down.
Transport exclusion: the framing that certain groups are structurally locked out of mobility, making it a design and equity problem rather than a personal one.
Participatory design activity: hands-on co-design where excluded participants generate their own solutions for a technology they've never used.
[Relevance to argument]: This is the closest paper in terms of method, with the same technology (AVs), same kind of underserved population, same multi-workshop approach with a not-yet-experienced technology. ### [Participatory Method] Tan, Y., Soh, K.X., Zhang, R., Lee, J., Meng, H., Sen, B., & Lee, Y.-C. (2025) - Empowering social service with AI: Insights from a participatory design study with practitioners.
[Central claim]: This paper argues that a not-yet-embedded technology in a social service setting must be co-designed with frontline practitioners through workshops, because only that process surfaces their real concerns; over lost skills, overreliance, and threats to the human core of the profession; early enough to shape the design.
[Key concepts or framework]: Two-stage participatory design: The study’s overarching methodology, Stage 1 being co-design workshops to build a prototype, and Stage 2 including contextual inquiry where practitioners test it on real cases.
Boundary work: the line practitioners draw between "monotonous" administrative labor and the "professional, knowledge-based" work they see as core to their identity, which new tech threatens to cross.
Overreliance: the risk that workers lean on the tool's output without critical checking, especially junior staff.
[Relevance to argument]: This is our strongest domain-matched precedent covering social service, the same context SECS operates in. It supports our thread by showing that workshops with frontline social service workers reliably surface exactly the relational-trust and human-connection concerns that our own Seniors & Volunteers and Stakeholder workshops raised, reinforcing that these values only emerge through participatory methods, not from studying the technology alone.
[Gap or limitation]: All participants came from two agencies in a single country, and the study is about AI casework tools rather than mobility, so its value for us is in the method and the pattern of practitioner concern, not in the specific technology.

### [Participatory Method] Aranda-Muñoz, Á., Bozic Yams, N., & Carlgren, L. (2025) - Co-experiential futuring: Where speculative design and arts meet futures studies
[Central claim]: This paper argues that combining speculative design with artistic intervention across a series of workshops actively strengthens participants' sense of agency over their own future, meaning speculative workshops don't just collect opinions, they change participants' relationship to a future being shaped around them.
[Key concepts or framework]: Speculative Design: A practice that uses design not to solve immediate problems, but to create "diegetic prototypes" that open up discourse and allow people to question preferable futures
Design Fiction: A form of speculative design that uses a collection of artifacts to build a fictional world, helping participants explore alternative realities and assess current practices
Experiential futures: futures that participants feel and enact in the moment, rather than discuss in the abstract.
[Relevance to argument]: Though not about AVs, this supports our thread by proving the workshop-plus-speculation method reliably works in a completely different domain (healthcare-worker training), which answers the obvious objection that our method only worked because of our specific context. ### [Participatory Method] Liao, Q.V., & Muller, M. (2019) - Enabling value sensitive AI systems through participatory design fictions
[Central claim]: This paper argues that when a technology isn't yet part of people's everyday lives, the only adequate way to inquire into stakeholders' values is to fuse participatory design with design fiction, because neither studying current practice nor speculation alone can surface values about a technology that doesn't yet exist.
[Key concepts or framework]: Value Sensitive Design (VSD): an established HCI approach for accounting for human values throughout the design process, not just performance and usability.
Participatory Design Fictions (PDF): their core framework: a method for soliciting stakeholder input through "strategically incomplete fictions", stories that establish a setting and characters but end prematurely at a critical decision point.
Grounded Theory: A qualitative analysis method used to identify conceptual categories and value themes within the collected stories
[Relevance to argument]: This is the paper that names and justifies our exact method, just applied to AI rather than AVs - we could not interview pantry users about a non-existent AV, so we did what this paper prescribes: brought in real stakeholders and gave them a speculative version to react to. It is our direct answer to "why not just run interviews."
[Gap or limitation]: Its own case study is very small (six volunteers and one short workshop), so it offers a well-argued rationale for the method but only preliminary evidence that it works at scale.

### [Participatory Method] Barendregt, L., Bendor, R., & van Eekelen, B.F. (2024) - Public participation in futuring: A systematic literature review.
[Central claim]: This paper argues that participatory futuring, despite its democratic rhetoric, mostly includes only experts and professionals rather than affected publics, and that even when publics are involved the process is often not designed to genuinely empower them.
[Key concepts or framework]: Participatory futuring: collective activities in which people imagine and deliberate over possible futures.
Arnstein's ladder of citizen participation: a classic scale of how much real power participants hold, from token consultation to genuine control; they argue futuring can empower in ways this ladder misses.
The 'subject of the future': an identity position that could make participation more generative, by casting participants as authors of the future rather than data sources.
[Relevance to argument]: This review supplies field-level evidence that our project's decision to include pantry users and volunteers directly, not only SECS staff or experts, is a genuinely stronger and less common move, which backs our "passive users vs stakeholders" sub-point with more than opinion. It positions our participatory choice as filling a documented gap in the field.
[Gap or limitation]: As a review of published academic work, it captures only what has been formally written up, missing informal or community-led engagement that never reached publication.

### [Participatory Method] Forlano, L., & Mathew, A. (2014) - From design fiction to design friction: Speculative and participatory design of values-embedded urban technology.
[Central claim]: This paper argues that combining speculative and participatory design for urban technology should aim for "friction"; deliberate moments of disagreement, resistance, and reframing among stakeholders; because it is precisely in that friction that people's embedded values become visible and contestable.
[Key concepts or framework]: Design friction: deliberately built-in resistance or disagreement in a design process, treated as productive rather than as a failure.
Participatory Workshops: A series of five-hour workshops where participants used design fiction to move past today's technical constraints and focus on human values.
Designing Policy Toolkit: A large-format visual poster that serves as a "boundary object" to bridge the gap between scientific experts, policymakers, and citizens. It explains concepts like value-sensitive design and provides a methodology for collaborative workshops.
[Relevance to argument]: This gives a name and a rationale to the "breakdown moments" in our own workshops, like the points where our speculative AV concept met resistance, confusion, or reframing. It supports our thread by reframing that friction not as a flaw in the method but as the exact mechanism through which community values (and their misalignment with efficiency logic) surface.
[Gap or limitation]: Its stakeholders are mixed policy actors rather than a specifically underserved community, so it doesn't address the power asymmetries between participant types that a food-pantry context makes central.

### [Participatory Method] Omori, M., & Lim, Y. (2025) - Design futuring through diegetic prototypes: Provoking value reflection on emerging technologies.
[Central claim]: This paper argues that a single believable speculative prototype, even a fake but plausible product website, can function as a public-facing tool that provokes people into reflecting on their own values around an emerging technology, rather than merely eliciting their opinion of a product.
[Key concepts or framework]: Diegetic prototype: a fictional artifact built to feel like it belongs to a real, believable world (borrowed from Kirby's film-studies concept).
Value Elicitation: using the prototype specifically to surface concerns like privacy, autonomy, and control, not usability.
Speculative Realism: treating design as inquiry that expands what's imaginable rather than solving a predefined problem.
[Relevance to argument]: This paper isolates the specific mechanism our other sources only imply, that is - how a speculative artifact pulls values out of people, which directly justifies our decision to build a speculative AV provocation rather than survey or interview participants. It's less direct (a single case study, no community workshop), but it names the engine that makes our provocation work.
[Gap or limitation]: It's a single design case with no formal participant study attached, so it can't tell us how reactions were gathered or how the method holds up with a real, diverse community.

### [Participatory Method] Sörries, P., Leimstädtner, D., & Müller-Birn, C. (2024) - Advocating values through meaningful participation: Introducing a method to elicit and analyze values for enriching data donation practices in healthcare.
[Central claim]: This paper argues that a structured, multi-phase participatory workshop can reliably convert vulnerable stakeholders' stated values into concrete design requirements, demonstrating that value elicitation through workshops produces actionable design direction rather than just discussion.
[Key concepts or framework]: Participatory Value Elicitation: a four-phase workshop method built specifically to draw out and then translate participants' values into requirements.
Value Sensitive Design (VSD): the same foundational values-in-design framework Liao & Muller build on, here operationalized in a real workshop.
Reflective Design: deliberately surfacing the usually-unconscious values a technology carries by having participants reflect on alternatives.
[Relevance to argument]: This is the clearest evidence on our list that the workshop-to-values-to-design pipeline actually closes; that values surfaced in workshops become real design requirements, not just talk. It directly supports our thread's implicit promise: that the community concerns our workshops surfaced (equitable access, relational trust, flexibility) can concretely inform how AV-related infrastructure should be designed.
[Gap or limitation]: Only three workshops in a single healthcare and legal context (GDPR-bound Germany), so the four-phase method's transferability to a mobility or food-pantry setting is untested.

### [Participatory Method] Wong, R.Y., Mulligan, D.K., Van Wyk, E., Pierce, J., & Chuang, J. (2017) - Eliciting values reflections by engaging privacy futures using design workbooks.
[Central claim]: This paper argues that a workbook of speculative design fictions can serve as a deliberate "values lever" that surfaces people's underlying social values about an emerging technology, meaning speculative artifacts are an active tool for eliciting values, not just a passive stimulus to react to.
[Key concepts or framework]: Speculative Design Workbook: a collection of speculative design proposals assembled to help people explore and reflect on a design space, not to pitch a real product.
Values levers (from Shilton): anything that shifts a conversation to foreground values and treat them as central to design; they argue their workbooks acted as one.
Privacy By Design (PBD): embedding privacy protections into products during design, ratherthan retroactively
[Relevance to argument]: This is our strongest, award-winning proof that a speculative artifact deliberately elicits values rather than merely provoking a reaction, which is the precise justification for why we built a speculative AV provocation instead of asking participants directly. It anchors the "why this method" core of our thread.
[Gap or limitation]: Participants were an expert population (graduate students training as tech professionals), not an underserved community, so we can't assume the workbook effect transfers identically to food pantry users without adaptation.
