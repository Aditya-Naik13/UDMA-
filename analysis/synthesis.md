# Transcript Synthesis: Five Anchor Questions

## About this document

- Corpus: 34 transcripts, coded individually in `analysis/coding-notes/`.
  - 2 co-design workshops: Workshop 1 (TMF internal team affinity map on AVs),
    Workshop 3 (multi-organization stakeholder synthesis map on AVs). No
    Workshop 2 in the set.
  - 3 facilitated community sessions: Hope Food Pantry focus group (Insight
    Box, "ideal meal"), Ministries of Love focus group (same exercise), Old
    Bethel community weaver interview (persona and stakeholder mapping, dated
    4 November 2024).
  - 29 one-to-one interviews: 6 Hope pantry clients and 22 Southeast Community
    Pantry (SEC) clients, all conducted by an AI agent ("Alex"); plus 1
    Ministries of Love client interview conducted by a human. 5 of the SEC
    interviews were conducted in Spanish and appear here in English
    translation.
- Method: two-stage coding. Per-transcript notes against the five anchor
  questions (Values; Capabilities and Constraints; Meanings and Practices;
  Misalignment; Provocation), then this synthesis across the notes. Anchor
  questions set by the research lead.
- Fieldwork dates: the Old Bethel interview is dated November 2024. The focus
  groups reference an upcoming Thanksgiving and a "December 1" event and read
  as late 2025. The AI interviews carry only times of day. Exact dates and
  participant demographics are mostly not in the transcripts.
- Caveats:
  - The AI interviews are formulaic and often thin. Many are truncated
    (Hope 02, 05, 06; SEC 07, 08, 09, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19). Auto-transcription
    noise is heavy throughout, and several interviews were conducted while the
    client was driving, managing children, or working the pantry line.
  - The 5 Spanish transcripts are translations, so quotes from them are not in
    the participant's own words.
  - Anchor 5 (Provocation) is under-evidenced. Only 4 transcripts contain a
    real speculative prompt, and only 2 of those (the Hope and Ministries of
    Love focus groups) put it to community members. The other two are the team
    and stakeholders talking about AVs among themselves.
  - Site skew: SEC interviewees overwhelmingly drive and live close, so the
    SEC sample under-represents the carless. The transportation crisis is
    sharper in the Hope, Old Bethel, and Ministries of Love material and among
    the few carless participants (the Ministries of Love walker; SEC 09, who
    lost a vehicle).
  - Only 2 of the "5 older community-weaver transcripts" the team referenced
    are present (Old Bethel is one; the workshops are internal, not
    community-weaver). This gap is flagged for the team.
  - No homebound person was interviewed directly. They are spoken about in
    every community session but never speak.
- All participant names are withheld. People are referred to by site, role,
  and speaker or turn locator.

## 1. Values

### 1.1 Mutual aid is already the infrastructure

Across all three community sessions, clients describe running their own
distribution networks for people who cannot get to a pantry. This is routine,
unpaid, and organized, not occasional.

- "I make sure she's got something at least four or five times a week... Monday
  through Thursday I'm at her house." (focus-group-hope, 00:20:31, Speaker 8,
  on a 93 year old homebound neighbor)
- "it ain't just for me and my family it's other people in our neighborhood
  that know there's homeless... somebody comes by every day and somebody's
  getting something." (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:46:25 and 00:46:46,
  Speaker 3)
- "I have a neighbor that I usually take a box to she's 85 and a cancer
  survivor. They can't really drive no more." (focus-group-old-bethel,
  01:10:04)
- "right now I have to play Uber and Lyft to my older grandkids that are having
  transportation issues." (sec-participant-01, turn 18, Interviewee)

The values inside this work are non-interrogation and showing up: "Nothing. I
don't ask questions. Look at you saying nothing." (focus-group-hope, 00:20:56,
Speaker 5); "I don't ask questions, I just show up." (focus-group-hope,
00:40:10, Speaker 8). The research lead names this back to the room as
infrastructure: "instead of being afraid of the technology, I'm looking at the
great asset in this community, carrying each other... using the terms
infrastructure." (focus-group-hope, 00:54:18, Speaker 2).

### 1.2 Dignity, shame, and the deservingness reflex

Shame around asking for help is the most consistent emotional finding in the
corpus, and it crosses race, language, and culture.

- "welfare, food, you're in the pantry, you're dirty. It's almost like the
  mental ill still, stigma." (focus-group-old-bethel, 55:51)
- "one of my Burmese family said. My family would be ashamed. I would be
  ashamed if they knew I was coming here." (focus-group-old-bethel, 55:05)
- "To be looked down upon." (sec-participant-06, 15:52:46, on what makes asking
  hard)
- "I get sad or feel a bit down because I don't know much English."
  (sec-participant-15-sp, 15:28:02; translated)

A specific form of this is self-rationing. Clients hold back in case someone
else needs it more, three separate Hope interviewees unprompted:

- "Whether somebody else needs it more." (hope-participant-03, 08:45:30)
- "I would rather somebody else to have it, especially if they have children."
  (hope-participant-04, 08:44:20)
- "that makes my day better, to know that you're there for people when they're
  in their time of need." (hope-participant-01, 09:14:46)

Against this, one clear counter-current: clients also describe the church
pantry as a place where shame is lower because it is read as compassionate and
non-judgmental. "they feel comfortable with Old Bethel because they know it's a
church pantry... more compassionate, more understanding, more empathetic."
(focus-group-old-bethel, 01:20:40); "they do not discriminate against you, they
see you as another family united with the community." (sec-participant-11-sp,
15:13:44; translated).

### 1.3 Being known is what earns trust and exceptions

Clients repeatedly attribute their comfort, and any flexibility they receive,
to staff who recognize them personally.

- "she knows I come. So when she has to go to work on those Saturdays, she
  allows me to pick it up for her and my dad. And some people are like, nope,
  they got to be here." (focus-group-hope, 00:51:15, Speaker 8)
- "we come here all the time. You see us all the time. You know us, who we
  are." (focus-group-hope, 00:51:13, Speaker 8)
- "They come out and they talk to you. They introduce themselves and they let
  you know what they have available." (hope-participant-04, 08:42:40)
- "here with them, they give you confidence, they listen to you... anything,
  they let me know through a message or text message or WhatsApp message."
  (sec-participant-15-sp, 15:27:26; translated)

For Spanish-speaking clients this becomes a request for relationship over
translation, covered in 4.6.

### 1.4 Faith and family are the named sources of endurance

Asked what keeps them going, participants overwhelmingly name God, family, or
both. This is close to universal in the interview set.

- "Our back, and he'll never leave us or stray us in the wrong direction."
  (hope-participant-01, 09:12:53)
- "the spirit of Jesus. And then also just like the people that have... wanted
  me to continue." (ministries-of-love-interview-walker, 03:42, Speaker 2)
- "My will to survive." then "My son." (hope-participant-02, 09:09:21 and
  09:09:31)
- "Family." then "Willingness to help." (sec-participant-14, 15:24:06 and
  15:24:20)

One participant broke this pattern: asked what helps her keep going, she said
"Nothing." (sec-participant-14, 15:20:56). Worth keeping as a check on the
resilience framing: not everyone has a reservoir.

### 1.5 Stability, not abundance, is the aspiration

When participants describe what they actually want, it is consistently modest
and framed as steadiness, not more.

- "they just want stability. They want to be able to. Have what they need, have
  it, and then be able to get what they want."
  (ministries-of-love-interview-walker, 03:18, Speaker 2)
- "I want freedom from being broke." (focus-group-hope, 00:14:09, Speaker 1)
- "Income predictability." (hope-participant-03, 08:46:25)
- "everyone dreams of a quiet place, their house, their car, well, a better
  living situation." (sec-participant-04-sp, 16:13:05; translated)

In the "ideal meal" exercise, several Hope participants wrote "nothing" on
their plates and named emotional and financial needs instead: "mashed potatoes
it's filling they fulfill me completely... everything else is nothing."
(focus-group-hope, 00:09:15, Speaker 1).

### 1.6 The organizer and team side runs on a different value set

Where the community sessions center care, recognition, and steadiness, the two
co-design workshops center efficiency, and one stakeholder session names the
gap explicitly.

- Team, Workshop 1: "How might we use AVs to reduce logistic company cost
  (labor, time, asset) to improve the distribution efficiency"
  (workshop-1-codesign, CLUSTER: Workforce Optimization, HMW); "AV can
  anticipate the shortage of food. No communication from human."
  (workshop-1-codesign, CLUSTER: Communication, Idea)
- Stakeholders, Workshop 3: "Social service meaning relational engagement not
  transactional" and "AV barrier: it needs to be intentional about the
  relation" (workshop-3-codesign, cluster of the same name)

Workshop 1's clusters split roughly into a large logistics-and-optimization
family and a small people-and-place family, with at least one participant
repeatedly reintroducing "food pantry as a social hub" against the pull of the
logistics discussion (workshop-1-codesign, cross-bucket notes). The
misalignment the paper describes is visible inside the design team itself, not
only in outside vendors.

## 2. Capabilities and Constraints

### 2.1 Capabilities the community already holds

- **Care distribution networks and neighborhood knowledge.** Clients can name
  who is homebound, which blocks lack sidewalks, which stores overcharge, and
  they route food accordingly. "we've been in this neighborhood all our life."
  (focus-group-hope, 00:50:12); "They quick with the word of mouth. And that's
  the original internet right there." (focus-group-old-bethel, 01:13:45).
- **Multilingualism.** Refugee clients "speak six, seven, five, six languages.
  We speak one." (focus-group-old-bethel, 11:50). SEC clients are bilingual
  and want to build on it (sec-participant-10, 15:33:44).
- **Self-taught skill and resourcefulness.** A client taught herself to cook
  over five disabled years at home and now converts a diabetic household's
  meals (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:30:13, Speaker 7). Clients coach
  each other on Community Compass, 211, produce days, cheap eyeglasses, and
  property-tax fixes (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:23:13, Speaker 3).
- **Trip-chaining.** Clients fold the pantry into trips they already make:
  school pickup, the Boys and Girls Club, a clinic. "I go, pick up my children
  from school, and at the same time, I pick up my food." (sec-participant-11-sp,
  15:13:20; translated).
- **Persistence and slow mobility gain.** "At first, I came on foot and then I
  started coming in a small car... after a year I managed to buy a small car."
  (sec-participant-15-sp, 15:25:38; translated).
- **Credentials and aspirations already in hand.** Clients list college
  degrees, "one credit from my master" (focus-group-hope, 00:24:42), 18 years
  running a licensed daycare (focus-group-hope, 00:36:15), and near-complete
  medical-assistant licenses (sec-participant-10, 15:33:44). Three separate SEC
  clients name a medical career as the goal (sec-participant-02,
  sec-participant-10, sec-participant-20).

### 2.2 Systemic constraints that blunt those capabilities

- **Benefit cliffs and income volatility.** "my dad was $74 over. $74 over, so
  you don't get nothing." (focus-group-hope, 00:41:07); "we didn't qualify for
  food stamps." (sec-participant-01, turn 5); hours "from 40 hours to 20 hours"
  (focus-group-hope, 00:18:12).
- **The working poor.** Several clients are employed and still cannot cover
  food: "Job pay." (sec-participant-19, 16:13:16); "thank God, I am working,
  but not every day." (sec-participant-04-sp, 16:13:58); "Work." as the reason
  there is no rest (hope-participant-06, 15:33:44).
- **A car is not the same as mobility.** Owning a vehicle still leaves clients
  blocked by fuel cost ("not having gas all the time", sec-participant-12,
  15:05:44), car payments ("sometimes the payment is difficult",
  sec-participant-04-sp, 16:14:15), reliability ("Miss Ruby's got a car, but
  it's broke almost every other month", focus-group-old-bethel, 42:34), and
  losing the vehicle entirely, which ended one client's engagement with
  services ("No transportation." sec-participant-09, 15:37:48).
- **No car, no license, no nearby transit.** "I don't know how to drive. I've
  never had a license." (focus-group-hope, 00:47:10); "it's a lot of bus stops
  and a lot of times the bus stops to your destination is not close."
  (focus-group-hope, 00:41:41); "the closest all these around here. It's like a
  two-hour walk for me." (ministries-of-love-interview-walker, 08:15, Speaker
  2).
- **Transit that does not fit a grocery trip.** "I usually tend to buy bulk,
  and so like carrying all that onto the bus... it messed with my nerves a
  bit." (ministries-of-love-interview-walker, 09:05, Speaker 2).
- **Childcare cost exceeds the wage.** "she can't afford a babysitter, got to
  stay home." (focus-group-old-bethel, 37:51).
- **Language.** "The language." (sec-participant-03-sp, 16:19:37; translated),
  named as the barrier both to work and to seeking help across all 5 Spanish
  transcripts and the Old Bethel intake description.
- **Digital access.** "how many people don't have a bank account. phone or
  internet or Wi-Fi." (focus-group-old-bethel, 33:36); "not everybody has the
  phone." (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:45:16).
- **Disability and anxiety.** "They expect me to do things I'm not physically
  capable of." (sec-participant-14, 15:21:45); "Being around others that I'm
  not familiar with." (sec-participant-05, 15:58:30).
- **Food deserts with predatory pricing.** "the prices... significantly higher
  ... because they know people who don't have a car don't have a choice."
  (focus-group-hope, 00:45:43).
- **The load.** Even clients who can walk any distance cannot carry the return
  trip: "the most challenging part is going back because I have the bags."
  (ministries-of-love-interview-walker, 01:46, Speaker 2); "especially in the
  wintertime because it's cold." (focus-group-hope, 00:47:56).

### 2.3 Capability-and-constraint pairs that recur

- Wants part-time work, but is the family's sole driver and caregiver, so
  cannot (sec-participant-01, turn 18).
- Can walk 30 minutes or 2 hours, but cannot carry a bulk shop or a case of
  water home (ministries-of-love-interview-walker).
- Used every wraparound service, then lost transportation and dropped all of
  them, with no remote alternative (sec-participant-09).
- Trained toward a mental-health support job, blocked from rehire by a 2020
  record the hiring system surfaces (focus-group-hope, 00:31:04).
- Near a medical-assistant credential, blocked by education cost and student
  loans (sec-participant-02, sec-participant-10, sec-participant-20).

## 3. Meanings and Practices

### 3.1 What mobility means

- **Mobility is caregiving.** For many participants, almost every trip is on
  someone else's behalf: driving grandchildren, delivering to the homebound,
  picking up for two or three households. A grandmother whose daughter died in
  a car crash now drives her grandchildren everywhere (sec-participant-01).
- **Getting there is dignity.** The trip to the pantry is bound up with pride,
  shame, and being seen, not just with distance (section 1.2).
- **For the homebound, the delivery is the human contact.** Named by a planner,
  not a client: "Delivery person might be the only point of human contact the
  home bound people might have." (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: Home Bound);
  "Indygo: home delivery might be the only personal interaction."
  (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: Social service meaning relational engagement).
- **Mobility is hard-won progress.** Buying a small car after a year on foot is
  described as "striving to be someone better in life." (sec-participant-15-sp,
  15:30:03; translated).

### 3.2 How mobility is practiced

- **Trip-chaining is universal.** The pantry works when it sits on a route the
  client already drives: the school run, the Boys and Girls Club, a clinic
  (sec-participant-01, sec-participant-11-sp, sec-participant-03-sp).
- **Multi-pantry circuits.** Regular clients visit two or three pantries a week
  to assemble enough food. "she's coming to at least three pantries, two or
  three times a week." (focus-group-old-bethel, 27:26).
- **Redistribution circuits.** One client wakes at 6 a.m. to stand in line,
  then delivers to homebound neighbors (focus-group-hope, 00:49:41). Another
  cooks pantry surplus into daily meals handed out at the door
  (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:46:46).
- **Walking with a stroller as a cart**, cutting through a Dollar General
  parking lot because there is no sidewalk (focus-group-old-bethel, 01:08:57).
- **Pickup from inside the car.** At Hope the line is a line of vehicles; staff
  come out to the car (focus-group-hope, 00:48:11; hope-participant-02,
  09:08:02).
- **Order online, then drive to collect.** SEC runs an account-and-order
  website; the "trip" now includes a digital leg (sec-participant-21,
  sec-participant-15-sp).
- **Information mobility.** Much of every community session is people briefing
  each other on benefit changes, which pantry stopped stocking meat, a proposed
  shelter, a data center, job news.

### 3.3 Informal and care-driven mobility as its own finding

The community has already built the delivery and proxy-pickup network that most
pantry rules forbid (section 4.1). It is invisible to the formal system, and in
one case a data-collection rule made it more invisible: the Old Bethel weaver
was told to stop recording client nationality, which he had been using to plan
interpreter coverage (focus-group-old-bethel, 25:51). The capability exists;
the system does not see it or count it.

## 4. Misalignment

Ranked by weight of evidence across the corpus. Each names the efficiency or
legibility logic on the system side and the community value it costs.

### 4.1 In-person self-collection rules versus the homebound

**System logic:** require the beneficiary to appear in person, with ID, to
prevent fraud and keep the count legible. **Cost:** it directly excludes the
homebound people the community is trying to feed, and criminalizes the informal
network that fills the gap. **Framework value:** flexibility for variable need,
and equitable access.

- "She can't get out and get this. But then you can't take it to her because
  the person has to be here to get the benefits." (focus-group-hope, 00:19:42,
  Speaker 8)
- "So how can you help somebody that can't get out to help themselves, but then
  they say they got to be here in person." (focus-group-hope, 00:19:56)
- the rule's rationale, in a client's words: "that's because people take
  advantage and mess it up for the people who really need to pick it up for
  other people." (focus-group-hope, 00:51:45)
- Hope is praised specifically for bending it: "it's one of the only ones that
  allow you to get something for somebody that can't come." (focus-group-hope,
  00:51:15)
- "Homebound people miss out on transportation ideations." (workshop-3-codesign,
  cluster: Home Bound)

### 4.2 Rationing mechanisms experienced as unfair or unpredictable

**System logic:** cap frequency, randomize order, or move reservations online
to manage demand that has tripled and to be "fair." **Cost:** clients lose the
ability to plan, lose an afternoon, or lose out entirely. **Framework value:**
flexibility for variable need.

- Twice-a-month cap: "Now they've kind of restricted it... you do two times a
  month. But when it was like you could... twice a week... the need was that
  strong." (focus-group-old-bethel, 27:00)
- Once-a-month: "What, once a week? No, once a month." (sec-participant-22,
  16:39:27)
- Card-shuffler lottery: "it ain't even like first come first serve anymore...
  a card shuffler... you can be the first ones in line and not get out. Until
  six o'clock at night." (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:22:13, Speaker 7)
- App runs out: "when I enter the application, there's almost nothing,
  sometimes it seems like people don't give space for another person to take."
  (sec-participant-15-sp, 15:30:28; translated)
- "Whenever I have the opportunity, whenever I have the opportunity, chances to
  go." (sec-participant-13-sp, 15:14:56; translated), against a fixed schedule
  and cap.

### 4.3 Standardized boxes versus choice and dietary need

**System logic:** pre-pack a uniform box to move 400 families through in the
time that once served 100. **Cost:** clients lose selection, and health-
restricted clients get food they should not eat. **Framework value:**
flexibility for variable need, and dignity.

- "in the old days they would come in and shop... Now we just put it in the box
  and move on. Because... the volume got so much... we couldn't just keep
  letting them stay in there." (focus-group-old-bethel, 15:03)
- "people still think they in Kroger or something. Checking the prices."
  (focus-group-old-bethel, 15:33)
- "you cannot choose food items like we choose... it's not healthy... most of
  it's like starchy." (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:21:02, Speaker 7)

**Contrary evidence:** SEC lets clients choose, and it is repeatedly praised on
both dignity and waste grounds. "You could choose your own items... So we
didn't get stuff we didn't need and not use it." (sec-participant-02, turns 3
and 4); "they have a lot of food options." (sec-participant-22, 16:40:18). SEC
also runs a stocked clothing program that clients value the same way
(sec-participant-22, 16:41:37). Choice is a working design feature where it
exists.

### 4.4 Scheduling and hours redistribute who is served

**System logic:** fixed distribution windows and appointment slots.
**Cost/effect:** they serve the person with a predictable schedule and fail the
person with variable hours. This is not simply bad; it moves the burden.
**Framework value:** flexibility for variable need.

- Fails the variable-hours worker: "Food pantry works on Saturday mornings -
  many families work at that time." (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: Time);
  "Being able to schedule when you need to." (sec-participant-02, turn 12); "it
  would be good to have someone who can assist us... Quick." (sec-participant-13-sp,
  15:17:25; translated).
- Serves the fixed-shift worker: "I'm usually coming here right after work." /
  "My appointment time." / "That I don't have to miss work." (sec-participant-16,
  15:53:46, 15:53:59, 15:54:21).

### 4.5 Wraparound services are invisible and un-navigable

**System logic:** offer job coaching, financial guidance, education, and
childcare help alongside food. **Cost:** most clients do not know these exist,
and when told, cannot find a usable path in. What they want is concrete
(application links, interview connections, "what is available and how to obtain
it"), not "coaching." **Framework value:** equitable access to information.

- Did not know the services existed: sec-participant-05, 06, 10, 12, 13-sp, 17,
  19, 20, and the Spanish clients 03-sp and 04-sp. This is the single most
  repeated finding in the SEC set.
- What they actually want: "The one place they have the interview for get a job
  or the link for where I can apply." (sec-participant-10, 15:34:10);
  "Understanding. What finances are available, how to go about inquiring about
  those, what's needed." (sec-participant-21, 16:38:23).
- The website does not describe them: "It's not really described on the website
  very well. I would say that I'm not at the right, you know, location on the
  website." (sec-participant-21, 16:39:01).
- Referrals go unanswered: "I had took in information and I never heard back
  from them." (sec-participant-01, turn 13).

### 4.6 Language: translation versus relationship

**System logic:** provide a translator at the counter when needed. **Cost:**
for Spanish-speaking clients the language issue is a trust issue, not a
transaction issue, and an interpreter does not build trust. **Framework
value:** relational trust, and equitable access.

- "They always have a translator, but it would be good to have someone who can
  assist us in Spanish a bit and teach us to speak a bit of English."
  (sec-participant-04-sp, 16:14:37; translated)
- "that they speak a little Spanish... which makes you feel reliable, that at
  least the person understands what you are saying." (sec-participant-15-sp,
  15:29:04; translated)
- "having more people who speak Spanish." (sec-participant-04-sp, 16:28:20;
  translated)
- Named in 4 Spanish transcripts (03-sp, 04-sp, 11-sp, 15-sp).
- Related legibility failure: the Old Bethel weaver was told to stop recording
  client nationality, which he used to plan interpreter coverage
  (focus-group-old-bethel, 25:51).

### 4.7 Digitization assumes a device, a data plan, and literacy

**System logic:** move resource navigation, job applications, and pantry
ordering online. **Cost:** it locks out clients without a phone, wifi, or the
literacy for a digital form. **Framework value:** equitable access.

- "how you gonna... he was like, there's no paper application? I said, no, I
  haven't seen one in years." (focus-group-old-bethel, 01:27:21)
- "if you go to the community cupboards online, on your phone, a lot of people
  have smartphones." answered later by "not everybody has the phone."
  (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:23:13 and 00:45:16, Speaker 3)
- SEC's order-online-then-collect model works for the digitally fluent
  (sec-participant-21) and hides everything past the food order.

### 4.8 Programs assume an abled, unanxious, group-comfortable body

**System logic:** deliver wraparound help as in-person group programming.
**Cost:** it excludes clients with a disability or social anxiety.
**Framework value:** equitable access, and flexibility for variable need.

- "They expect me to do things I'm not physically capable of."
  (sec-participant-14, 15:21:45)
- "Being around others that I'm not familiar with." (sec-participant-05,
  15:58:30)
- "Some people aren't as understanding of your situation." (sec-participant-06,
  15:54:09)

**Contrary evidence:** the drive-through model, impersonal to some, is a real
accessibility feature for the anxious client: "Having the sense of not being
around a lot of people and being able to just drive through." (sec-participant-05,
15:55:08).

### 4.9 Eligibility gates: zip code, benefit thresholds, documents

**System logic:** define a service area and verify eligibility. **Cost:**
clients are turned away from better-stocked pantries by where they live, and
gated at intake by producing documents. **Framework value:** equitable access.

- "That's out east. You have to live in their area codes." / "You can't go
  under the zip code." (focus-group-hope, 00:43:35 and 00:43:39)
- "everybody's got a card, their number, show their ID, move on."
  (focus-group-old-bethel, 14:46)

### 4.10 The pantry addresses food, not the cost of reaching it

**System logic:** provide food. **Cost:** the trip itself, gas, a car payment,
$25 to $30 for an Uber against a $2 bus that does not stop nearby, is left to
the client. **Framework value:** equitable access.

- "They ain't getting no Uber, no Lyft... that Uber and Lyft, that's $25, $30."
  (focus-group-old-bethel, 01:06:45)
- "not having gas all the time." (sec-participant-12, 15:05:44)
- Adding a bus stop near the Old Bethel pantry is stalled behind an IndyGo
  feasibility and sustainability study (focus-group-old-bethel, 42:11).
- The reflex answer to "what would help," even from clients who report an easy
  trip: "Transportations." / "Bus lines." (sec-participant-22, 16:44:58 and
  16:45:09); "reliable transportation." (sec-participant-09, 15:38:05).

### 4.11 Planning happens without the community

**System logic:** plan routes, sites, and services through institutional
process. **Cost:** decisions land on people who had no say, and obvious fixes
wait on studies. **Framework value:** community participation.

- "Neighbor have ideas imposed on them. Their needs are not accommodated."
  (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: People centered, Input/decision making)
- "the people that got the money, they're going to do it anyhow. It don't
  matter if you protest or not." (focus-group-hope, 00:45:02, Speaker 7, on a
  proposed shelter and a data center)
- institutions renaming away from "community" and "service": a university unit
  became "workforce development and community engagement" ("you took the
  community out", focus-group-hope, 00:57:03); FSSA became "family and social
  resources" (focus-group-old-bethel, 52:17).

### 4.12 The AI interview instrument enacts the transactional mode it is studying

**System logic:** automate the client-feedback interview. **Cost:** across the
Hope and SEC interviews the AI loops scripted lines, cuts clients off,
mishears, cannot answer basic operational questions, drops its connection,
interviews people who are driving or minding children or are themselves
children, and openly declines to give information. It extracts and gives
nothing back. **Framework value:** relational trust.

- loops: "There are no right or wrong answers here..." four times in the first
  minute (hope-participant-01, 09:08:31 to 09:09:30); the client says "Same
  question." (hope-participant-05, 08:33:28)
- cannot answer operational questions: "How often can you come even the one
  online?" / "I don't have that information." (sec-participant-06, 15:57:06 and
  15:57:15)
- declines to inform: "Of course, I won't give you specific options. Rather,
  what ideas come to your mind." (sec-participant-13-sp, 15:19:28; translated)
- drops connection mid-interview, twice: "Alex, are you still there?"
  (sec-participant-21, 16:36:38)
- interviews someone at the wheel: "Green light. Why are we breaking?"
  (sec-participant-16, 15:58:41)
- interviews a child with questions about job coaching and financial confidence
  (sec-participant-18)
- pushes to extend on a parent trying to leave: "I am in an interview,
  children, please understand... I can't finish it because I am going to drive
  now." (sec-participant-11-sp, 15:20:15; translated)
- a client's doubt that it matters: "I don't know if somebody's going to
  actually listen or whatever." (sec-participant-01, turn 21)
- a first-time client who arrives already wanting to give ("I'm give you a
  flyer", "we're having a lunch and learn on... Medicare") is met only with
  "Understood." (hope-participant-01, 09:08:48 and 09:09:04)

**Contrary evidence:** the same automation, conducted in Spanish, lowered a
language barrier. "Very good, very understandable." (sec-participant-03-sp,
16:21:35; translated); "the best of all, in Spanish." (sec-participant-15-sp,
15:31:15; translated). One client named the voice interface itself as the
positive: "Voice talk." (sec-participant-20, 16:30:06). The critique is about
relational fit and context-awareness, not about technology as such.

### 4.13 Efficiency as the design team's own first instinct

**System logic:** the Workshop 1 team, unprompted, reached for route
optimization, driver reduction, 24/7 operation, waste extraction, face
verification, and "AV as friend" for lonely seniors. **Cost:** a relational
need (companionship) is reframed as a feature, and the human is designed out.
**Framework value:** relational trust.

- "Senior citizens are lonely, become friends with AV." (workshop-1-codesign,
  CLUSTER: Logistics, Idea)
- "Should have some sort of face verification to ensure food is handed to the
  right person." (workshop-1-codesign, CLUSTER: Privacy, Idea)

A minority of the same team pushed back: "How might we ensure there is a human
element/touch with AVs used as a social service." (workshop-1-codesign,
CLUSTER: Logistics, HMW).

### 4.14 Success measured as adoption, not burden

**System logic:** Workshop 3 named, on the board, that "Biggest ROI of AV as a
social service is the adoption rate of AV" and connected it to "Serving
Automakers interest in building infrastructure." **Cost:** it points the design
toward uptake numbers rather than whether anyone's load got lighter.
**Framework value:** Mobility as Social Service versus product-adoption logic.

- against: "new metrics that measure the weights of burden and capture the
  ultimate benefit to the end user." (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: ROI)

## 5. Provocation

Coverage is thin and should not be overstated. Real speculative prompts appear
in only 4 transcripts: Workshop 1 (team), Workshop 3 (stakeholders), and the
Hope and Ministries of Love focus groups (community). The AI interviews contain
no AV prompt; the closest is participants' reactions to being interviewed by an
AI, coded as adjacent throughout.

### 5.1 Community reactions to the driverless-vehicle prompt

Both focus groups were asked a version of "if there were a car with no driver,
available 24/7, donated to your community, what would you do with it."

- Cautious yes, framed immediately through a personal mobility gap, not
  logistics: "I would take a car. You know why?... I don't know how to drive.
  I've never had a license. If a car could drive me... like the Uber, I could
  put the address in and I'll just sit there and go, la, listen to my music,
  dance, and the car just be rolling." (focus-group-hope, 00:47:10, Speaker 7)
- Use cases named by clients: reaching a destination the bus does not, and not
  standing in the cold. "it's a lot of bus stops and a lot of times the bus
  stops to your destination is not close." (focus-group-hope, 00:47:41);
  "especially in the wintertime because it's cold." (focus-group-hope,
  00:47:56).
- A concrete design condition, offered by a community member: bound the
  destinations. "you need to put in a destination... limit the destinations to
  like pantries and grocery store." (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:49:07
  to 00:49:19, Speakers 3 and 7)
- A concern rooted in neighborhood knowledge: "it would have to be on issues...
  they could be using it for drugs... so we're in the hood so if we make a
  vehicle accessible it may not just be used for needs but for other things."
  (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:48:34, Speaker 3)
- The backdrop: both focus groups discussed automation taking jobs (White
  Castle kiosks, "Julia your assistant"; federal job reclassification), and
  the AV question landed in that anxiety. "it's not fair for all of us to be
  dominated, replaced by all this kind of technology conversation."
  (focus-group-hope, 00:53:35, agreed to by participants).
- Repeatedly, the low-tech version of the same need is what participants
  actually propose: "bus vouchers would be good for people, too."
  (focus-group-hope, 00:48:32); "it would be good to pass out some bus passes."
  (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:48:11).

### 5.2 Stakeholder reactions (Workshop 3)

- Skepticism that AV is a general solution: "AV cannot be designed for
  everyone. It's not the next big solution." (workshop-3-codesign, cluster:
  Personalized services); "All personas are not the same."
- Regional and institutional reluctance to adopt at all
  (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: Awareness).
- The most concrete positive use case: small AVs as micro-transit shuttles for
  the first and last mile between transit lines and pantries
  (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: Micro transit).
- The binding condition: "it needs to be intentional about the relation";
  "Maintaining the human relation." (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: Social
  service meaning relational engagement not transactional).

### 5.3 Team ideas (Workshop 1)

Mostly logistics (delivery optimization, refrigerated AV storage for pantries,
last-mile drones, labor reduction). A minority recurring theme: AVs for people
not food (rides for homebound neighbors and aging volunteers), mobile common
spaces that park and wait for neighbors, and "food pantry as a social hub"
reintroduced more than once (workshop-1-codesign).

## Design implications (tentative)

Every item below is a hypothesis for the team to test and debate, not a
settled specification. Each names the transcript evidence it rests on and what
would confirm or kill it.

1. **Design for the return-with-goods leg, not the trip.** The recurring
   mobility problem is carrying food home, not covering distance
   (ministries-of-love-interview-walker; focus-group-hope winter line;
   transit-does-not-fit-bulk). *Confirm:* a delivery or carry-home pilot
   reduces missed pickups among carless and load-limited clients. *Kill:*
   clients say the trip out, not the trip back, is the barrier.

2. **Replace the in-person self-collection rule with an authorized proxy or
   delivery path for the homebound.** The community already runs this network
   informally and is penalized for it (section 4.1). *Confirm:* a formal proxy
   system reaches homebound people currently served only by neighbors. *Kill:*
   fraud or diversion rises to a level that outweighs the reach gained.

3. **Bound the destinations.** If a shared vehicle or ride service is offered,
   community members themselves proposed limiting it to pantries and grocery
   stores (focus-group-ministries-of-love, 00:49:19). *Confirm:* clients accept
   and use a destination-limited service. *Kill:* the limit makes it useless
   for the trip-chaining that clients actually do.

4. **Keep a human at the point of contact.** For the homebound, the delivery is
   the human interaction of the day (workshop-3-codesign); clients ask for "a
   smile" (hope-participant-06, 15:34:41) and are unsettled by the AI
   interviewer (section 4.12). *Confirm:* a human-accompanied delivery scores
   higher on trust and continued use than a contactless one. *Kill:* clients
   prefer contactless for privacy or speed (see item 5).

5. **Offer both a low-contact and a high-contact path, chosen by the client.**
   The drive-through serves the socially anxious (sec-participant-05); the
   personal greeting serves the isolated (hope-participant-04). The same design
   choice helps one and fails the other. *Confirm:* uptake splits meaningfully
   when both are offered. *Kill:* almost everyone picks the same one.

6. **Route distribution through trips that already happen.** A young SEC
   respondent proposed after-school pickup at the child's own school, with the
   child as courier (sec-participant-18, 16:14:47); trip-chaining onto the
   school run is already near-universal (section 2.1). *Confirm:* a school-site
   or school-run pickup raises participation among working parents. *Kill:*
   schools cannot host it, or parents will not send children as couriers.

7. **Fund the first and last mile with what people keep asking for.** Across
   the corpus the concrete requests are bus passes, gas cards, and a bus stop
   at the pantry, not an AV (sections 4.10, 5.1). Micro-transit shuttles to
   pantries are the one AV use case stakeholders endorsed (workshop-3-codesign).
   *Confirm:* a bus-pass or gas-card pilot changes attendance. *Kill:* clients
   have the fare and still cannot come, meaning the barrier is time or the
   load, not cost.

8. **Make the wraparound services a navigable, described, in-language thing.**
   Clients do not know the services exist, and want application links,
   appointment slots, and "who contacts you," not "coaching" (sections 4.5,
   4.6). SEC Participant 21's own questions are a ready-made spec for a
   services page. *Confirm:* a plain, translated services page with concrete
   next steps raises enrollment. *Kill:* enrollment stays flat, meaning the
   barrier is trust or capacity, not information.

9. **Protect predictability in whatever scheduling or reservation system is
   used.** Lotteries, frequency caps, and apps that run out are all experienced
   as unfair and unplannable (section 4.2). *Confirm:* a booked-slot system
   with held inventory reduces wasted trips and complaints. *Kill:* demand so
   exceeds supply that any system produces the same rationing pain.

10. **Measure by burden lifted, not adoption.** Adopt the stakeholders' own
    proposed frame: "weights of burden" and "benefit to the end user"
    (workshop-3-codesign, cluster: ROI), against adoption rate. *Confirm:* the
    team can define and collect a burden metric. *Kill:* no workable burden
    metric emerges and adoption remains the only measurable thing.

11. **Build it with the community.** The most consistent grievance in the
    corpus is imposition: "ideas imposed on them," things "put in our
    neighborhood," names with "community" removed (section 4.11). *Confirm:* a
    co-design process changes at least one substantive design decision. *Kill:*
    participation is run but changes nothing, confirming the community's
    suspicion.

## Mapping to the paper framework

### Equitable access

Strongly supported. Benefit cliffs (focus-group-hope, 00:41:07), zip-code
gates (focus-group-hope, 00:43:39), language (all 5 Spanish transcripts),
digital-only applications and ordering (focus-group-old-bethel, 01:27:21;
sec-participant-21), disability and anxiety exclusion from programs
(sec-participant-14, sec-participant-05), food deserts with predatory pricing
(focus-group-hope, 00:45:43), and the uncosted trip itself (section 4.10) all
show the service reaching some and not others by design. The wraparound
services being unknown to most SEC clients (section 4.5) is the clearest single
access failure.

### Relational trust

Strongly supported. Being personally known is what earns clients flexibility
and comfort (section 1.3). Where the system substitutes a transaction for a
relationship, clients notice: a translator instead of a Spanish-speaking
staffer (section 4.6), an unanswered referral (sec-participant-01, turn 13), an
AI interviewer that loops and cannot answer questions (section 4.12), and a
design instinct to make a machine the lonely senior's "friend"
(workshop-1-codesign). Stakeholders name the value directly: "Social service
meaning relational engagement not transactional." (workshop-3-codesign).

### Flexibility for variable need

Strongly supported. Frequency caps, lotteries, apps that run out (section 4.2),
pre-packed boxes (section 4.3), fixed distribution windows and appointment
slots (section 4.4), and programs that assume a stable schedule, an abled body,
and reliable transportation (sections 4.8, 4.9, and sec-participant-09) all
force fluctuating, opportunistic need into fixed slots. Clients articulate the
value from their side: "Income predictability." (hope-participant-03); "they
just want stability." (ministries-of-love-interview-walker); "Whenever I have
the opportunity." (sec-participant-13-sp).

### Mobility as Social Service

Partly supported, and there is a positive existence proof. SEC functions for
some Spanish-speaking parents as a genuine hub: school meetings, English
classes, financial counseling, food, and clothing, all in one place, reached on
the school-pickup trip (sec-participant-11-sp, sec-participant-15-sp). That is
close to the MaSS ideal already, minus the vehicle. The governance tension the
paper describes is visible in Workshop 3: an AV social service whose "biggest
ROI" is "adoption rate" and whose infrastructure investment is framed as
"serving automakers interest" is being pulled toward product logic, against a
stakeholder counter-proposal to measure "weights of burden."

### Community participation

Supported, with an important complication. The transcripts are full of
decisions landing on people who had no say: planning "imposed on them"
(workshop-3-codesign), a shelter and a data center "put in our neighborhood"
regardless of protest (focus-group-hope, 00:45:02), a bus stop stalled behind a
study while people walk with boxes (focus-group-old-bethel, 42:11), and
institutions dropping "community" and "service" from their names (sections
1.6, 4.11). The complication: in this corpus the actor doing the imposing is
almost always the food-bank network, the funders, or the city, not an AV
vendor. The AV has not arrived. So the transcripts confirm that the pattern the
paper describes is real and pre-existing, but they do not yet test the paper's
specific claim about autonomous vehicles arriving without participation,
because no participant has experienced that.

### Where the transcripts complicate or contradict the argument

- **Not everyone experiences friction.** Several Hope and SEC clients report
  the pantry as sufficient and warm and ask only that it "stay the same"
  (hope-participant-02, 09:09:56; hope-participant-04; sec-participant-20,
  16:29:44). The misalignment findings are real but not universal.
- **Efficiency design sometimes serves the marginalized.** The drive-through
  helps the socially anxious (sec-participant-05); the appointment slot helps
  the fixed-shift worker (sec-participant-16); SEC's choice model and clothing
  program are praised (sec-participant-02, sec-participant-22); the
  Spanish-language AI interview lowered a language barrier (sec-participant-03-sp,
  sec-participant-15-sp). The paper should not treat efficiency and community
  value as always opposed.
- **The clearest imposition in the corpus is not technological.** It is the
  box model, the twice-a-month cap, the ID card, and the study requirement.
  This strengthens the paper's structural claim while weakening any
  tech-determinist version of it.
- **The people who would benefit most from an AV were never asked.** The
  carless walker (ministries-of-love-interview-walker) and the client who lost
  her car and dropped out of services (sec-participant-09) got no speculative
  prompt. The strongest case for the intervention is also the least evidenced.

## Open questions and gaps

- **Anchor 5 needs real fieldwork.** Only two community transcripts contain an
  AV prompt, and both are brief. Speculative sessions with carless clients,
  homebound people, and people who have lost transportation would give the
  design implications an evidence base they currently lack.
- **No homebound person was interviewed.** They are central to every session
  and silent in all of them. The paper's hardest case has no first-person
  voice in this corpus.
- **The missing transcripts.** Only 2 of the "5 older community-weaver
  transcripts" are present, and there is no Workshop 2. If they exist, they
  should be coded and folded in before the synthesis is treated as complete.
- **What happens after a referral.** The "never heard back" thread
  (sec-participant-01) is unresolved. Follow-up on referral outcomes would test
  whether the wraparound services fail at awareness, at intake, or at
  follow-through.
- **The SEC transportation picture is incomplete.** The SEC interview sample
  skews to car owners who live close. A sample that reached carless SEC clients
  would show whether SEC's "easy trip" pattern is real or an artifact of who
  answered.
- **The AI interview as method.** Roughly a third of the AI interviews are
  truncated or degraded. If the team continues using an AI interviewer, the
  reliability, context-awareness, and information-provision failures documented
  in section 4.12 need addressing, or the instrument will keep producing thin
  data and a poor client experience.
- **Dates and demographics.** The corpus does not consistently record when
  each session happened or who was in the room. Basic metadata would let the
  team weight and compare transcripts properly.
